## Traffic jams – no end in sight

 AA.

There are no easy answers to the problems of <T v="tình trạng ùn tắc giao thông" p="/ˈtræfɪk kənˈdʒestʃən/" t="collocation">traffic congestion</T>. Traffic congestion affects people throughout the world. Traffic jams cause <T v="khói bụi ô nhiễm" p="/smɒɡ/" t="noun">smog</T> in dozens of cities across both the <T v="thế giới phát triển và đang phát triển" p="/dɪˈveləpt ənd dɪˈveləpɪŋ wɜːld/" t="collocation">developed and developing world</T>. In the U.S., <T v="người đi làm/đi học hằng ngày" p="/kəˈmjuːtəz/" t="noun">commuters</T> spend an average of a full work week each year sitting in traffic, according to the Texas Transportation Institute.  BB.

The most promising technique for reducing city traffic is called <T v="chính sách thu phí chống ùn tắc" p="/kənˈdʒestʃən ˈpraɪsɪŋ/" t="academic phrase">congestion pricing</T>, whereby cities charge a <T v="phí cầu đường/phí vào khu vực" p="/təʊl/" t="noun">toll</T> to enter certain parts of town at certain times of day. In theory, if the toll is high enough, some drivers will cancel their trips or go by bus or train. And in practice it seems to work: Singapore, London and Stockholm have reduced traffic and pollution in <T v="trung tâm thành phố" p="/ˈsɪti ˈsentəz/" t="collocation">city centers</T> thanks to congestion pricing.

 CC.

Another way to reduce <T v="giao thông giờ cao điểm" p="/rʌʃ ˈaʊə ˈtræfɪk/" t="collocation">rush hour traffic</T> is for employers to implement <T v="giờ làm việc linh hoạt" p="/ˈfleksitaɪm/" t="noun">flexitime</T>, which lets employees travel to and from work at <T v="thời điểm ngoài giờ cao điểm" p="/ɒf piːk ˈtræfɪk taɪmz/" t="collocation">off-peak traffic times</T> to avoid the rush hour. Those who have to travel during busy times can do their part by <T v="đi chung xe" p="/ʃeə kɑːz/" t="collocation">sharing cars</T>. Employers can also allow more staff to <T v="làm việc từ xa" p="/ˌtelikəˈmjuːt/" t="verb">telecommute</T> (work from home) so as to <T v="giảm số lượng xe lưu thông" p="/kiːp kɑːz ɒf ðə rəʊd/" t="lexical pattern">keep more cars off the road altogether</T>.

 DD.

Some <T v="nhà quy hoạch đô thị" p="/ˈɜːbən ˈplænəz/" t="collocation">urban planners</T> still believe that the best way to <T v="giảm ùn tắc giao thông" p="/iːz ˈtræfɪk kənˈdʒestʃən/" t="collocation">ease traffic congestion</T> is to build more roads, especially roads that can take drivers around or over crowded city streets. But such techniques do not really <T v="giảm số lượng xe lưu thông" p="/kiːp kɑːz ɒf ðə rəʊd/" t="lexical pattern">keep cars off the road</T>; they only <T v="đáp ứng thêm nhiều xe" p="/əˈkɒmədeɪt mɔː kɑːz/" t="collocation">accommodate more of them</T>.

 EE.

Other, more <T v="có tư duy tiến bộ" p="/ˌfɔːwəd ˈθɪŋkɪŋ/" t="adjective">forward-thinking</T> planners know that more and more drivers and cars are <T v="đổ ra đường" p="/teɪk tə ðə rəʊdz/" t="idiomatic expression">taking to the roads</T> every day, and they are unwilling to encourage more <T v="phương tiện cá nhân" p="/ˈpraɪvət ˌɔːtəˈməʊbiːlz/" t="collocation">private automobiles</T> when <T v="giao thông công cộng" p="/ˈpʌblɪk ˈtrænspɔːt/" t="collocation">public transport</T> is so much better both for people and the environment. For this reason, the American government has decided to spend some $7 billion on helping to <T v="tăng công suất/sức chứa" p="/ɪnˈkriːs kəˈpæsəti/" t="collocation">increase capacity</T> on public transport systems and <T v="nâng cấp bằng công nghệ hiệu quả hơn" p="/ʌpˈɡreɪd wɪð mɔː ɪˈfɪʃənt tekˈnɒlədʒiz/" t="collocation">upgrade them with more efficient technologies</T>. But <T v="các nhà bảo vệ môi trường" p="/ɪnˌvaɪrənˈmentəlɪsts/" t="noun">environmentalists</T> complain that such <T v="nguồn tài trợ, ngân sách" p="/ˈfʌndɪŋ/" t="noun">funding</T> is tiny compared with the $50 billion being spent on roads and bridges.
