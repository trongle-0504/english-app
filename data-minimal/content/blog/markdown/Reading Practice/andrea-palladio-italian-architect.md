# Andrea Palladio: Italian Architect

A new exhibition celebrates Palladio’s architecture 500 years on.

Vicenza is a pleasant, prosperous city in the Veneto, 60 km west of Venice. Its grand families settled and farmed the area from the 16th century. But its <T v="điểm nổi tiếng chính" p="/ˌprɪnsəpəl kleɪm tə feɪm/" t="collocation">principal claim to fame</T> is Andrea Palladio, who is such an <T v="kiến trúc sư có ảnh hưởng" p="/ˌɪnfluˈenʃəl ˈɑːkɪtekt/" t="collocation">influential architect</T> that a <T v="phong cách tân cổ điển" p="/ˌniːəʊˈklæsɪkəl staɪl/" t="collocation">neoclassical style</T> is known as Palladian. The city is a permanent exhibition of some of his finest buildings, and as he was born — in Padua, to be precise — 500 years ago, the International Centre for the Study of Palladio’s Architecture has an excellent excuse for mounting <T v="cuộc triển lãm lớn" p="/la ɡrande ˈmɔːstra/" t="academic phrase">la grande mostra</T>, the big show.

The exhibition has the <T v="lợi thế đặc biệt" p="/ˈspeʃəl ədˈvɑːntɪdʒ/" t="collocation">special advantage</T> of being held in one of Palladio’s buildings, Palazzo Barbaran da Porto. Its <T v="mặt tiền táo bạo, nổi bật" p="/bəʊld fəˈsɑːd/" t="collocation">bold facade</T> is a mixture of <T v="kiểu xây đá thô trong kiến trúc" p="/ˌrʌstɪˈkeɪʃən/" t="noun">rustication</T> and decoration set between two rows of elegant columns. On the second floor the <T v="mái tam giác trang trí" p="/ˈpedɪmənt/" t="noun">pediments</T> are alternately curved or pointed, a <T v="đặc trưng kiểu Palladio" p="/pəˈleɪdiən ˈtreɪdmɑːk/" t="collocation">Palladian trademark</T>. The <T v="tỷ lệ hài hòa" p="/hɑːˈməʊniəs prəˈpɔːʃənz/" t="collocation">harmonious proportions</T> of the <T v="đại sảnh trung tâm" p="/ˈeɪtriəm/" t="noun">atrium</T> at the entrance lead through to a dramatic interior of fine fireplaces and painted ceilings. Palladio’s design is simple, clear and not over-crowded. The show has been organised on the same principles, according to Howard Burns, the <T v="nhà sử học kiến trúc" p="/ˌɑːkɪˈtektʃərəl hɪˈstɔːriən/" t="collocation">architectural historian</T> who <T v="đồng giám tuyển" p="/ˌkəʊ ˈkjʊəreɪtɪd/" t="verb">co-curated</T> it.

Palladio’s father was a miller who settled in Vicenza, where the young Andrea was <T v="được học việc với ai" p="/əˈprentɪst tə/" t="lexical pattern">apprenticed to</T> a skilled stonemason. How did a humble miller’s son become a <T v="kiến trúc sư nổi tiếng thế giới" p="/wɜːld rɪˈnaʊnd ˈɑːkɪtekt/" t="collocation">world renowned architect</T>? The answer in the exhibition is that, as a young man, Palladio **excelled at** carving decorative stonework on columns, doorways and fireplaces. He was plainly intelligent, and lucky enough to come across a rich <T v="người bảo trợ" p="/ˈpeɪtrən/" t="noun">patron</T>, Gian Giorgio Trissino, a landowner and scholar, who organised his education, taking him to Rome in the 1540s, where he studied the <T v="kiệt tác kiến trúc La Mã và Hy Lạp cổ điển" p="/ˈmɑːstəpiːsɪz əv ˈklæsɪkəl ˈrəʊmən ænd ɡriːk ˈɑːkɪtektʃə/" t="academic phrase">masterpieces of classical Roman and Greek architecture</T> and the work of other influential architects of the time, such as Donato Bramante and Raphael.

Burns argues that <T v="sự dịch chuyển địa vị xã hội" p="/ˌsəʊʃəl məʊˈbɪləti/" t="noun">social mobility</T> was also important. Entrepreneurs, prosperous from agriculture in the Veneto, <T v="thuê/giao cho ai thực hiện" p="/kəˈmɪʃən/" t="lexical pattern">commissioned</T> the promising local architect to design their country villas and their urban mansions. In Venice the aristocracy were anxious to <T v="thu hút, kết nạp" p="/kəʊˈɒpt/" t="verb">co-opt</T> talented artists, and Palladio was given the chance to design the buildings that have made him famous – the churches of San Giorgio Maggiore and the Redentore, both easy to admire because they can be seen from the city’s <T v="trung tâm lịch sử" p="/hɪˈstɒrɪkəl ˈsentə/" t="collocation">historical centre</T> across a stretch of water.

He tried his hand at bridges — his <T v="phiên bản chưa được xây dựng" p="/ˌʌnˈbɪlt ˈvɜːʃən/" t="collocation">unbuilt version</T> of the Rialto Bridge was decorated with the large <T v="mái tam giác trang trí" p="/ˈpedɪmənt/" t="noun">pediment</T> and columns of a temple — and, after a fire at the Ducal Palace, he offered an <T v="thiết kế thay thế" p="/ɔːlˈtɜːnətɪv dɪˈzaɪn/" t="collocation">alternative design</T> which bears an <T v="có nét giống kỳ lạ với" p="/beər ən ʌnˈkæni rɪˈzembləns tə/" t="lexical pattern">uncanny resemblance to</T> the Banqueting House in Whitehall in London. Since it was designed by Inigo Jones, Palladio’s first foreign <T v="môn đồ, người theo trường phái" p="/dɪˈsaɪpəl/" t="noun">disciple</T>, this is not as surprising as it sounds.

Jones, who visited Italy in 1614, bought a trunk full of the master’s <T v="bản vẽ kiến trúc" p="/ˌɑːkɪˈtektʃərəl ˈdrɔːɪŋz/" t="collocation">architectural drawings</T>; they passed through the hands of the Dukes of Burlington and Devonshire before settling at the Royal Institute of British Architects in 1894. Many are now on display at Palazzo Barbaran. What they show is how Palladio **drew on** the buildings of ancient Rome as models. The <T v="chủ đề chính" p="/ˈmeɪdʒə θiːm/" t="collocation">major theme</T> of both his rural and urban building was <T v="kiến trúc đền thờ" p="/ˈtempəl ˈɑːkɪtektʃə/" t="collocation">temple architecture</T>, with a strong pointed <T v="mái tam giác trang trí" p="/ˈpedɪmənt/" t="noun">pediment</T> supported by columns and approached by wide steps.

Palladio’s work for rich landowners <T v="khiến xa lánh" p="/ˈeɪliəneɪt/" t="verb">alienates</T> <T v="những nhà phê bình bảo thủ" p="/ˌʌnriːkənˈstrʌktɪd ˈkrɪtɪks/" t="collocation">unreconstructed critics</T> on the Italian left but among the papers in the show are designs for cheap housing in Venice. In the wider world, Palladio’s reputation has been <T v="nuôi dưỡng, phát triển" p="/ˈnɜːtʃə/" t="verb">nurtured by</T> a text he wrote and illustrated, “Quattro Libri dell’ Architettura”. His influence spread to St Petersburg and to Charlottesville in Virginia, where Thomas Jefferson <T v="thuê/giao cho ai thực hiện" p="/kəˈmɪʃən/" t="lexical pattern">commissioned</T> a Palladian villa he called Monticello.

Vicenza’s show contains <T v="mô hình chi tiết" p="/ˈdiːteɪld ˈmɒdəlz/" t="collocation">detailed models</T> of the major buildings and is <T v="được làm sinh động bởi" p="/ˈlevənd baɪ/" t="lexical pattern">leavened by</T> portraits of Palladio’s teachers and clients by Titian, Veronese and Tintoretto; the paintings of his Venetia buildings are all by Canaletto, no less. This is an <T v="triển lãm mang tính nghiêm túc, không chiều thị hiếu" p="/ʌnˈkɒmprəmaɪzɪŋ ˌeksɪˈbɪʃən/" t="collocation">uncompromising exhibition</T>; many of the drawings are small and faint, and there are no <T v="hoạt động phụ, trò phụ" p="/ˈsaɪdʃəʊ/" t="noun">sideshows</T> for children, but the impact of <T v="đường nét hài hòa" p="/hɑːˈməʊniəs laɪnz/" t="collocation">harmonious lines</T> and <T v="tỷ lệ tạo cảm giác dễ chịu" p="/ˈsætɪsfaɪɪŋ prəˈpɔːʃənz/" t="collocation">satisfying proportions</T> is to impart in a viewer a feeling of <T v="sự bình yên dễ chịu" p="/bəˈnevələnt kɑːm/" t="collocation">benevolent calm</T>. Palladio is history’s most <T v="kiến trúc sư mang tính chữa lành" p="/ˌθerəˈpjuːtɪk ˈɑːkɪtekt/" t="collocation">therapeutic architect</T>.

“Palladio, 500 Anni: La Grande Mostra” is at Palazzo Barbaran da Porto, Vicenza, until January 6th 2009. The exhibition continues at the Royal Academy of Arts, London, from January 31st to April 13th, and travels afterwards to Barcelona and Madrid.
