# Alexander Henderson (1831-1913)

Born in Scotland, Henderson emigrated to Canada in 1855 and became a well-known <T v="nhiếp ảnh gia phong cảnh" p="/ˈlændskeɪp fəˈtɒɡrəfə/" t="collocation">landscape photographer</T>.

Alexander Henderson was born in Scotland in 1831 and was the son of a successful merchant. His grandfather, also called Alexander, had founded the family business, and later became the first chairman of the National Bank of Scotland. The family had <T v="sở hữu nhiều đất đai rộng lớn" p="/ɪkˈstensɪv ˈlændˌhəʊldɪŋz/" t="collocation">extensive landholdings</T> in Scotland. Besides its residence in Edinburgh, it owned Press Estate, 650 acres of farmland about 35 miles southeast of the city. The family often stayed at Press Castle, the large mansion on the northern edge of the property, and Alexander spent much of his childhood in the area, playing on the beach near Eyemouth or fishing in the streams nearby.

Even after he went to school at Murcheston Academy on the outskirts of Edinburgh, Henderson returned to Press at weekends. In 1849 he began a three-year <T v="thời gian học việc" p="/əˈprentɪʃɪp/" t="noun">apprenticeship</T> to become an accountant. Although he never liked the <T v="triển vọng, khả năng xảy ra" p="/ˈprɒspekt əv/" t="lexical pattern">prospect of</T> a business career, he stayed with it to please his family. In October 1855, however, he <T v="di cư tới" p="/ˈemɪɡreɪt tə/" t="lexical pattern">emigrated to</T> Canada with his wife Agnes Elder Robertson and they settled in Montreal. Henderson learned photography in Montreal around the year 1857 and quickly took it up as a <T v="người nghiệp dư nhưng nghiêm túc/chuyên sâu" p="/ˈsɪəriəs ˈæmətə/" t="collocation">serious amateur</T>. He became a <T v="bạn thân và đồng nghiệp" p="/ˈpɜːsənəl frend ænd ˈkɒliːɡ/" t="collocation">personal friend and colleague</T> of the Scottish-Canadian photographer William Notman. The two men made a <T v="chuyến đi chụp ảnh" p="/ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk ɪkˈskɜːʃən/" t="collocation">photographic excursion</T> to Niagara Falls in 1860 and they cooperated on <T v="các thí nghiệm với" p="/ɪkˈsperɪmənts wɪð/" t="lexical pattern">experiments with</T> magnesium flares as a source of <T v="ánh sáng nhân tạo" p="/ˌɑːtɪˈfɪʃəl laɪt/" t="collocation">artificial light</T> in 1865. They belonged to the same societies and were among the <T v="thành viên sáng lập" p="/ˈfaʊndɪŋ ˈmembəz/" t="collocation">founding members</T> of the Art Association of Montreal. Henderson acted as chairman of the association's first meeting, which was held in Notman's studio on 11 January 1860.

In spite of their friendship, their styles of photography were quite different. While Notman's landscapes were noted for their <T v="chủ nghĩa hiện thực táo bạo" p="/bəʊld ˈrɪəlɪzəm/" t="collocation">bold realism</T>, Henderson for the first 20 years of his career produced <T v="hình ảnh mang tính lãng mạn" p="/rəʊˈmæntɪk ˈɪmɪdʒɪz/" t="collocation">romantic images</T>, showing the strong influence of the British landscape tradition. His <T v="tiến bộ nghệ thuật và kỹ thuật" p="/ɑːˈtɪstɪk ænd ˈteknɪkəl ˈprəʊɡres/" t="collocation">artistic and technical progress</T> was rapid and in 1865 he published his first <T v="bộ sưu tập lớn" p="/ˈmeɪdʒə kəˈlekʃən/" t="collocation">major collection</T> of landscape photographs. The publication had <T v="số lượng lưu hành hạn chế" p="/ˈlɪmɪtɪd ˌsɜːkjəˈleɪʃən/" t="collocation">limited circulation</T> (only seven copies have ever been found), and was called Canadian Views and Studies. The contents of each copy vary significantly and have proved a <T v="nguồn hữu ích để đánh giá" p="/ˈjuːsfəl sɔːs fə ɪˈvæljueɪtɪŋ/" t="academic phrase">useful source for evaluating</T> Henderson's early work.

In 1866, he gave up his business to open a photographic studio, advertising himself as a portrait and landscape photographer. From about 1870 he dropped portraiture to <T v="chuyên về" p="/ˈspeʃəlaɪz ɪn/" t="lexical pattern">specialize in</T> landscape photography and other views. His numerous photographs of city life revealed in street scenes, houses, and markets are alive with <T v="hoạt động của con người" p="/ˈhjuːmən ækˈtɪvəti/" t="collocation">human activity</T>, and although his favourite subject was landscape he usually <T v="xây dựng bố cục xoay quanh" p="/kəmˈpəʊz siːnz əˈraʊnd/" t="lexical pattern">composed his scenes around</T> such human pursuits as farming the land, cutting ice on a river, or sailing down a woodland stream. There was <T v="nhu cầu đủ lớn đối với" p="/səˈfɪʃənt dɪˈmɑːnd fə/" t="lexical pattern">sufficient demand for</T> these types of scenes and others he took depicting the lumber trade, steamboats and waterfalls to enable him to make a living. There was little <T v="hoạt động nhiếp ảnh nghiệp dư cạnh tranh" p="/kəmˈpiːtɪŋ ˈhɒbi ɔː ˈæmətə fəˈtɒɡrəfi/" t="academic phrase">competing hobby or amateur photography</T> before the late 1880s because of the <T v="kỹ thuật tốn thời gian" p="/ˈtaɪm kənˌsjuːmɪŋ tekˈniːks/" t="collocation">time-consuming techniques</T> involved and the weight of the equipment. Henderson frequently <T v="triển lãm ảnh" p="/ɪɡˈzɪbɪt ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːfs/" t="collocation">exhibited his photographs</T> in Montreal and abroad, in London, Edinburgh, Dublin, Paris, New York, and Philadelphia. He met with <T v="thành công lớn hơn" p="/ˈɡreɪtə səkˈses/" t="collocation">greater success</T> in 1877 and 1878 in New York when he won first prizes in the exhibition held by E and HT Anthony and Company for landscapes using the <T v="quy trình in ảnh Lambertype" p="/ˈlæmbətaɪp ˈprəʊses/" t="noun">Lambertype process</T>. In 1878 his work won second prize at the <T v="triển lãm thế giới" p="/wɜːld ˌeksɪˈbɪʃən/" t="collocation">world exhibition</T> in Paris.

In the 1870s and 1880s Henderson travelled widely throughout Quebec and Ontario, in Canada, <T v="ghi lại, tư liệu hóa" p="/ˈdɒkjʊmentɪŋ/" t="verb">documenting</T> the major cities of the two provinces and many of the villages in Quebec. He was especially fond of the <T v="vùng hoang dã" p="/ˈwɪldənəs/" t="noun">wilderness</T> and often travelled by canoe on the Blanche, du Lievre, and other noted eastern rivers. He went on several occasions to the Maritimes and in 1872 he sailed by yacht along the lower north shore of the St Lawrence River. That same year, while in the lower St Lawrence River region, he took some photographs of the <T v="việc xây dựng" p="/kənˈstrʌkʃən əv/" t="lexical pattern">construction of</T> the Intercolonial Railway. This undertaking led in 1875 to a <T v="đơn đặt hàng/công việc được giao từ" p="/kəˈmɪʃən frəm/" t="lexical pattern">commission from</T> the railway to record the <T v="các công trình chính" p="/ˈprɪnsəpəl ˈstrʌktʃəz/" t="collocation">principal structures</T> along the almost-completed line connecting Montreal to Halifax. Commissions from other railways followed. In 1876 he photographed bridges on the Quebec, Montreal, Ottawa and Occidental Railway between Montreal and Ottawa. In 1885 he went west along the Canadian Pacific Railway (CPR) as far as Rogers Pass in British Columbia, where he took photographs of the mountains and the <T v="tiến độ xây dựng" p="/ˈprəʊɡres əv kənˈstrʌkʃən/" t="collocation">progress of construction</T>.

In 1892 Henderson accepted a <T v="vị trí toàn thời gian" p="/ˌfʊl ˈtaɪm pəˈzɪʃən/" t="collocation">full-time position</T> with the CPR as manager of a photographic department which he was to set up and administer. His duties included spending four months in the field each year. That summer he made his second trip west, photographing extensively along the railway line as far as Victoria. He continued in this post until 1897, when he <T v="nghỉ hưu hoàn toàn khỏi" p="/rɪˈtaɪə kəmˈpliːtli frəm/" t="lexical pattern">retired completely from</T> photography.

When Henderson died in 1913, his <T v="bộ sưu tập khổng lồ" p="/hjuːdʒ kəˈlekʃən/" t="collocation">huge collection</T> of glass negatives was stored in the basement of his house. Today collections of his work are held at the National Archives of Canada, Ottawa, and the McCord Museum of Canadian History, Montreal.
