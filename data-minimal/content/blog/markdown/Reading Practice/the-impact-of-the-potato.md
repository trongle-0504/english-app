# The impact of the Potato

Jeff Chapman relates the story of history the most important vegetable

The potato was first <T v="được canh tác" p="/ˈkʌltɪveɪtɪd/" t="verb">cultivated</T> in South America between three and seven thousand years ago, though scientists believe they may have grown wild in the region as long as 13,000 years ago. The <T v="mô hình phân bố di truyền của khoai tây" p="/dʒəˈnetɪk ˈpætənz əv pəˈteɪtəʊ ˌdɪstrɪˈbjuːʃən/" t="collocation">genetic patterns of potato distribution</T> indicate that the potato probably originated in the <T v="khu vực miền núi trung tâm phía tây" p="/ˈmaʊntɪnəs west ˈsentrəl ˈriːdʒən/" t="collocation">mountainous west-central region</T> of the continent.

Early Spanish chroniclers who misused the Indian word batata (sweet potato) as the name for the potato noted the importance of the <T v="củ (thực vật)" p="/ˈtjuːbə/" t="noun">tuber</T> to the Incan Empire. The Incas had learned to <T v="bảo quản để lưu trữ" p="/prɪˈzɜːv fə ˈstɔːrɪdʒ/" t="lexical pattern">preserve the potato for storage</T> by <T v="làm khô và nghiền nhuyễn" p="/ˌdiːhaɪˈdreɪtɪŋ ənd mæʃɪŋ/" t="collocation">dehydrating and mashing</T> potatoes into a substance called Chuchu could be stored in a room for up to 10 years, providing excellent insurance against possible <T v="mất mùa" p="/krɒp ˈfeɪljəz/" t="collocation">crop failures</T>. As well as using the food as a <T v="cây lương thực chính" p="/ˈsteɪpl krɒp/" t="collocation">staple crop</T>, the Incas thought potatoes made childbirth easier and used it to treat injuries.

The Spanish <T v="những kẻ chinh phục Tây Ban Nha" p="/kɒnˈkwɪstədɔːz/" t="noun">conquistadors</T> first encountered the potato when they arrived in Peru in 1532 in search of gold, and noted Inca miners eating Chuchu. At the time the Spaniards failed to realize that the potato represented a far more important treasure than either silver or gold, but they did gradually begin to use potatoes as <T v="khẩu phần cơ bản trên tàu" p="/ˈbeɪsɪk ˈræʃənz əˈbɔːd ʃɪps/" t="collocation">basic rations aboard their ships</T>. After the arrival of the potato in Spain in 1570，a few Spanish farmers began to <T v="canh tác quy mô nhỏ" p="/ˈkʌltɪveɪt ɒn ə smɔːl skeɪl/" t="lexical pattern">cultivate them on a small scale</T>, mostly as food for livestock.

Throughout Europe, potatoes were regarded with <T v="sự nghi ngờ, ác cảm và sợ hãi" p="/səˈspɪʃən dɪsˈteɪst ənd fɪə/" t="collocation">suspicion, distaste and fear</T>. Generally considered to be <T v="không phù hợp cho con người tiêu thụ" p="/ʌnˈfɪt fə ˈhjuːmən kənˈsʌmpʃən/" t="collocation">unfit for human consumption</T>, they were used only as <T v="thức ăn gia súc" p="/ˈænɪməl ˈfɒdə/" t="collocation">animal fodder</T> and sustenance for the starving. In northern Europe, potatoes were primarily grown in <T v="vườn thực vật" p="/bəˈtænɪkl ˈɡɑːdənz/" t="collocation">botanical gardens</T> as an <T v="thứ mới lạ ngoại lai" p="/ɪɡˈzɒtɪk ˈnɒvlti/" t="collocation">exotic novelty</T>. Even peasants refused to eat from a plant that produced ugly, misshapen tubers and that had come from a <T v="nền văn minh ngoại đạo" p="/ˈhiːðən ˌsɪvəlaɪˈzeɪʃən/" t="collocation">heathen civilization</T>. Some felt that the potato plant’s resemblance to plants in the <T v="họ cà độc dược" p="/ˈnaɪtʃeɪd ˈfæməli/" t="collocation">nightshade family</T> hinted that it was the creation of witches or devils.

In meat-loving England, farmers and urban workers regarded potatoes with <T v="sự cực kỳ chán ghét" p="/ɪkˈstriːm dɪsˈteɪst/" t="collocation">extreme distaste</T>. In 1662, the Royal Society recommended the <T v="việc trồng khoai tây" p="/ˌkʌltɪˈveɪʃən əv ðə ˈtjuːbə/" t="collocation">cultivation of the tuber</T> to the English government and the nation, but this recommendation had little impact. Potatoes did not become a staple until, during the <T v="tình trạng thiếu lương thực liên quan đến" p="/fuːd ˈʃɔːtɪdʒɪz əˈsəʊsieɪtɪd wɪð/" t="lexical pattern">food shortages associated with</T> the Revolutionary Wars, the English government began to officially encourage potato cultivation. In 1795, the Board of Agriculture issued a pamphlet entitled “Hints Respecting the Culture and Use of Potatoes”； this was followed shortly by <T v="bài xã luận ủng hộ khoai tây" p="/prəʊ pəˈteɪtəʊ ˌedɪˈtɔːriəlz/" t="collocation">pro-potato editorials</T> and potato recipes in The Times. Gradually, the <T v="tầng lớp thấp" p="/ˈləʊə ˈklɑːsɪz/" t="noun phrase">lower classes</T> began to follow the lead of the <T v="tầng lớp thượng lưu" p="/ˈʌpə ˈklɑːsɪz/" t="noun phrase">upper classes</T>.

A similar pattern emerged across the English Channel in the Netherlands, Belgium and France. While the potato slowly <T v="dần trở nên phổ biến" p="/ɡeɪn ɡraʊnd/" t="idiomatic expression">gained ground</T> in eastern France (where it was often the only crop remaining after <T v="lính cướp bóc tàn phá" p="/məˈrɔːdɪŋ ˈsəʊldʒəz ˈplʌndəd/" t="collocation">marauding soldiers plundered</T> wheat fields and vineyards), it did not achieve <T v="sự chấp nhận rộng rãi" p="/ˌwaɪdspred əkˈseptəns/" t="collocation">widespread acceptance</T> until the late 1700s. The peasants remained suspicious, in spite of a 1771 paper from the Faculté de Paris **testifying that** the potato was not harmful but beneficial. The people began to overcome their distaste when the plant received the <T v="sự công nhận chính thức của hoàng gia" p="/ˈrɔɪəl siːl əv əˈpruːvl/" t="collocation">royal seal of approval</T>: Louis XVI began to sport a potato flower in his buttonhole, and Marie-Antoinette wore the purple potato blossom in her hair.

Frederick the Great of Prussia saw the potato’s <T v="tiềm năng giúp nuôi sống" p="/pəˈtenʃl tə help fiːd/" t="lexical pattern">potential to help feed</T> his nation and lower the price of bread, but faced the challenge of <T v="vượt qua định kiến về" p="/ˌəʊvəˈkʌm ˈpredʒudɪs əˈɡenst/" t="lexical pattern">overcoming the people’s prejudice against</T> the plant. When he issued a 1774 order for his subjects to grow potatoes as protection against famine, the town of Kolberg replied: “The things have neither smell nor taste, not even the dogs will eat them, so what use are they to us?” Trying a less direct approach to encourage his subjects to begin planting potatoes, Frederick used a bit of <T v="tâm lý đảo ngược" p="/rɪˈvɜːs saɪˈkɒlədʒi/" t="noun">reverse psychology</T>: he planted a royal field of potato plants and stationed a heavy guard to protect this field from thieves. Nearby peasants naturally assumed that anything worth guarding was worth stealing, and so <T v="lẻn vào cánh đồng" p="/sniːk ˈɪntuː ðə fiːld/" t="lexical pattern">snuck into the field</T> and snatched the plants for their home gardens. Of course, this was entirely <T v="phù hợp với" p="/ɪn laɪn wɪð/" t="lexical pattern">in line with</T> Frederick’s wishes.

Historians debate whether the potato was primarily a cause or an effect of the huge <T v="bùng nổ dân số" p="/ˌpɒpjəˈleɪʃən buːm/" t="collocation">population boom</T> in industrial-era England and Wales. Prior to 1800, the English diet had consisted primarily of meat, supplemented by bread, butter and cheese. Few vegetables were consumed, most vegetables being regarded as <T v="không có giá trị dinh dưỡng" p="/njuˈtrɪʃənəli ˈwɜːθləs/" t="collocation">nutritionally worthless</T> and potentially harmful. This view began to change gradually in the late 1700s. The Industrial Revolution was drawing an ever increasing percentage of the populace into <T v="các thành phố đông đúc" p="/ˈkraʊdɪd ˈsɪtiz/" t="collocation">crowded cities</T>, where only the richest could afford homes with ovens or coal storage rooms, and people were working 12-16 hour days which left them with little time or energy to prepare food. <T v="cho năng suất cao" p="/haɪ ˈjiːldɪŋ/" t="adjective">High yielding</T>, easily prepared potato crops were the obvious solution to England’s food problems.

Whereas most of their neighbors regarded the potato with suspicion and had to be persuaded to use it by the upper classes, the Irish peasantry <T v="củ (thực vật)" p="/ˈtjuːbə/" t="noun">embraced the tuber</T> more passionately than anyone since the Incas. The potato was <T v="rất phù hợp với" p="/biː wel ˈsuːtɪd tuː/" t="lexical pattern">well suited to</T> the Irish soil and climate, and its <T v="năng suất cao" p="/haɪ jiːld/" t="collocation">high yield</T> suited the most important concern of most Irish farmers: to feed their families.

The most dramatic example of the potato’s <T v="tiềm năng thay đổi mô hình dân số" p="/pəˈtenʃl tə ˈɔːltə ˌpɒpjəˈleɪʃən ˈpætənz/" t="collocation">potential to alter population patterns</T> occurred in Ireland, where the potato had become a staple by 1800. The Irish population doubled to eight million between 1780 and 1841, without any significant expansion of industry or reform of agricultural techniques beyond the <T v="sự canh tác rộng rãi" p="/ˌwaɪdspred ˌkʌltɪˈveɪʃən/" t="collocation">widespread cultivation</T> of the potato. Though Irish <T v="tập quán sở hữu đất đai" p="/ˈlændhəʊldɪŋ ˈpræktɪsɪz/" t="collocation">landholding practices</T> were primitive in comparison with those of England, the potato’s high yields allowed even the poorest farmers to produce more healthy food than they needed with scarcely any investment or hard labor. Even children could easily plant, harvest and cook potatoes, which of course required no threshing, curing or grinding. The abundance provided by potatoes greatly <T v="giảm tỷ lệ tử vong ở trẻ sơ sinh" p="/dɪˈkriːst ˈɪnfənt mɔːˈtæləti/" t="collocation">decreased infant mortality</T> and <T v="khuyến khích kết hôn sớm" p="/ɪnˈkʌrɪdʒd ˈɜːli ˈmærɪdʒ/" t="collocation">encouraged early marriage</T>.
