# Annotated Passage

Australian culture and culture shock

Sometimes work, study or a <T v="tinh thần thích khám phá" p="/sens əv ədˈventʃə/" t="collocation">sense of adventure</T> take us out of our <T v="môi trường quen thuộc" p="/fəˈmɪliə səˈraʊndɪŋz/" t="collocation">familiar surroundings</T> to go and live in a different culture. The experience can be difficult, even shocking. 

Almost everyone who studies, lives or works abroad has problems <T v="thích nghi với nền văn hóa mới" p="/əˈdʒʌst tə ə njuː ˈkʌltʃə/" t="lexical pattern">adjusting to a new culture</T>. This response is commonly referred to as '<T v="sốc văn hóa" p="/ˈkʌltʃə ʃɒk/" t="academic phrase">culture shock</T>'. Culture shock can be defined as 'the <T v="sự khó chịu về thể chất và tinh thần" p="/ˈfɪzɪkl ənd ɪˈməʊʃənl dɪsˈkʌmfət/" t="collocation">physical and emotional discomfort</T> a person experiences when entering a culture different from their own' (Weaver, 1993). 

For people moving to Australia, Price (2001) has identified certain <T v="các giá trị" p="/ˈvæljuːz/" t="noun">values</T> which may <T v="dẫn đến" p="/ɡɪv raɪz tuː/" t="lexical pattern">give rise to</T> culture shock. Firstly, he argues that Australians place a high value on <T v="sự độc lập và lựa chọn cá nhân" p="/ˌɪndɪˈpendəns ənd ˈpɜːsənl tʃɔɪs/" t="collocation">independence and personal choice</T>. This means that a teacher or course tutor will not tell students what to do, but will give them a number of options and suggest they <T v="tìm ra, tự giải quyết" p="/wɜːk aʊt/" t="phrasal verb">work out</T> which one is the best in their circumstances. It also means that they are expected to <T v="hành động" p="/teɪk ˈækʃən/" t="collocation">take action</T> if something goes wrong and <T v="chủ động tìm kiếm nguồn lực và hỗ trợ" p="/siːk aʊt rɪˈzɔːsɪz ənd səˈpɔːt/" t="collocation">seek out resources and support</T> for themselves. 

Australians are also prepared to <T v="chấp nhận nhiều quan điểm" p="/əkˈsept ə reɪndʒ əv əˈpɪnjənz/" t="collocation">accept a range of opinions</T> rather than believing there is one truth. This means that in an <T v="môi trường giáo dục" p="/ˌedʒuˈkeɪʃənl ˈsetɪŋ/" t="collocation">educational setting</T>, students will be expected to <T v="hình thành quan điểm riêng" p="/fɔːm wʌnz əʊn əˈpɪnjənz/" t="lexical pattern">form their own opinions</T> and defend the reasons for that point of view and the evidence for it. 

Price also comments that Australians are uncomfortable with <T v="sự khác biệt về địa vị" p="/ˈdɪfrənsɪz ɪn ˈsteɪtəs/" t="collocation">differences in status</T> and hence idealise the idea of <T v="đối xử công bằng với mọi người" p="/triːt ˈevriwʌn ˈiːkwəli/" t="lexical pattern">treating everyone equally</T>. An illustration of this is that most adult Australians call each other by their first names. This concern with equality means that Australians are uncomfortable taking anything too seriously and are even ready to <T v="tự trêu đùa bản thân" p="/dʒəʊk əˈbaʊt wʌnˈself/" t="lexical pattern">joke about themselves</T>. 

Australians believe that life should have a <T v="sự cân bằng giữa công việc và thời gian thư giãn" p="/ˈbæləns bɪˈtwiːn wɜːk ənd ˈleʒə taɪm/" t="collocation">balance between work and leisure time</T>. As a consequence, some students may be critical of others who they **perceive as** doing nothing but study. 

Australian <T v="quan niệm về quyền riêng tư" p="/ˈnəʊʃənz əv ˈprɪvəsi/" t="collocation">notions of privacy</T> mean that areas such as financial matters, appearance and relationships are only discussed with close friends. While people may volunteer such information, they may <T v="khó chịu vì ai làm gì" p="/rɪˈzent/" t="lexical pattern">resent someone asking</T> them unless the friendship is <T v="được thiết lập vững chắc" p="/ˈfɜːmli ɪˈstæblɪʃt/" t="collocation">firmly established</T>. Even then, it is considered very impolite to ask someone what they earn. With older people, it is also rude to ask how old they are, why they are not married or why they do not have children. It is also impolite to ask people how much they have paid for something, unless there is a very good reason for asking.

Kohls (1996) describes culture shock as a <T v="quá trình thay đổi" p="/ˈprəʊses əv tʃeɪndʒ/" t="collocation">process of change</T> marked by four <T v="các giai đoạn cơ bản" p="/ˈbeɪsɪk ˈsteɪdʒɪz/" t="collocation">basic stages</T>. During the first stage, the new arrival is excited to be in a new place, so this is often referred to as the "<T v="giai đoạn “trăng mật” ban đầu" p="/ˈhʌnimuːn steɪdʒ/" t="academic phrase">honeymoon stage</T>". Like a tourist, they are intrigued by all the new sights and sounds, new smells and tastes of their surroundings. They may have some problems, but usually, they accept them as just part of the <T v="sự mới lạ" p="/ˈnɒvlti/" t="noun">novelty</T>. At this point, it is the similarities that stand out, and it seems to the newcomer that people everywhere and their <T v="lối sống" p="/weɪ əv laɪf/" t="collocation">way of life</T> are very much alike. This period of <T v="cảm giác hưng phấn" p="/juːˈfɔːriə/" t="noun">euphoria</T> may last from a couple of weeks to a month, but the <T v="sự thất vọng" p="/ˈletdaʊn/" t="noun">letdown</T> is inevitable. 

During the second stage, known as the '<T v="giai đoạn phản kháng" p="/rɪˈdʒekʃən steɪdʒ/" t="academic phrase">rejection stage</T>', the newcomer starts to <T v="gặp khó khăn" p="/ɪkˈspɪəriəns ˈdɪfɪkəltiz/" t="collocation">experience difficulties</T> due to the differences between the new culture and the way they were <T v="quen với" p="/bi əˈkʌstəmd tuː/" t="lexical pattern">accustomed to</T> living. The initial enthusiasm turns into irritation, frustration, anger and depression, and these feelings may have the effect of people <T v="phản đối văn hóa mới" p="/rɪˈdʒekt ðə njuː ˈkʌltʃə/" t="collocation">rejecting the new culture</T> so that they notice only the things that cause them trouble, which they then complain about. In addition, they may feel <T v="nhớ nhà" p="/ˈhəʊmsɪk/" t="adjective">homesick</T>, bored, withdrawn and irritable during this period as well. 

Fortunately, most people gradually learn to <T v="thích nghi với văn hóa mới" p="/əˈdæpt tə ə njuː ˈkʌltʃə/" t="lexical pattern">adapt to the new culture</T> and move on to the third stage, known as '<T v="sự điều chỉnh và tái định hướng" p="/əˈdʒʌstmənt ənd riːˌɔːriənˈteɪʃən/" t="academic phrase">adjustment and reorientation</T>'. During this stage, a <T v="sự chuyển đổi xảy ra" p="/trænˈzɪʃən əˈkɜːz/" t="collocation">transition occurs</T> to a new optimistic attitude. As the newcomer begins to understand more of the new culture, they are able to <T v="hiểu các tín hiệu văn hóa tinh tế" p="/ɪnˈtɜːprət ˈsʌtl ˈkʌltʃərəl kluːz/" t="collocation">interpret subtle cultural clues</T> which passed by unnoticed earlier. Now things make more sense and the culture seems more familiar. As a result, they begin to <T v="phát triển kỹ năng giải quyết vấn đề" p="/dɪˈveləp ˈprɒbləm ˈsɒlvɪŋ skɪlz/" t="collocation">develop problem-solving skills</T>, and feelings of <T v="sự mất phương hướng và lo âu" p="/dɪsˌɔːriənˈteɪʃən ənd æŋˈzaɪəti/" t="collocation">disorientation and anxiety</T> no longer affect them. 

In Kohls's model, in the fourth stage, newcomers <T v="trải qua quá trình thích nghi" p="/ˌʌndəˈɡəʊ ə ˈprəʊses əv ˌædæpˈteɪʃən/" t="lexical pattern">undergo a process of adaptation</T>. They have settled into the new culture, and this results in a feeling of direction and self-confidence. They have accepted the new food, drinks, habits and customs and may even find themselves enjoying some of the very customs that <T v="làm ai khó chịu" p="/ˈbɒðəd/" t="lexical pattern">bothered them</T> so much previously. In addition, they realise that the new culture has good and bad things to offer and that no way is really better than another, just different.
