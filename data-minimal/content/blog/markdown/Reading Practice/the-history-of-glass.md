The history of glass

From our earliest origins, man has been making use of glass. Historians have discovered that a type of <T v="thủy tinh tự nhiên" p="/ˈnætʃrəl ɡlɑːs/" t="collocation">natural glass</T> - obsidian - formed in places such as the mouth of a volcano as a result of the <T v="nhiệt độ cực cao" p="/ɪnˈtens hiːt/" t="collocation">intense heat</T> of an eruption melting sand - was first used as tips for spears. Archaeologists have even found <T v="bằng chứng về thủy tinh nhân tạo" p="/ˈevɪdəns əv ˌmænˈmeɪd ɡlɑːs/" t="collocation">evidence of man-made glass</T> which dates back to 4000 BC; this took the form of <T v="lớp men phủ" p="/ˈɡleɪzɪz/" t="noun">glazes</T> used for coating stone beads. <T v="kỹ thuật thổi thủy tinh" p="/ɡlɑːs ˈbləʊɪŋ/" t="topic-specific term">Glass blowing</T> became the most common way to make glass containers from the first century BC. The glass made during this time was highly coloured due to the <T v="tạp chất của nguyên liệu" p="/ɪmˈpjʊərətiz əv rɔː məˈtɪəriəl/" t="collocation">impurities of the raw material</T>. In the first century AD, methods of creating <T v="thủy tinh không màu" p="/ˈkʌlələs ɡlɑːs/" t="collocation">colourless glass</T> were developed, which was then tinted by the addition of <T v="chất tạo màu" p="/ˈkʌlərɪŋ məˈtɪəriəlz/" t="collocation">colouring materials</T>. The secret of glass making was taken across Europe by the Romans during this century. However, they guarded the skills and technology required to make glass very closely, and it was not until their empire <T v="sụp đổ" p="/kəˈlæps/" t="verb">collapsed</T> in 476 AD that <T v="kiến thức chế tạo thủy tinh" p="/ɡlɑːs ˈmeɪkɪŋ ˈnɒlɪdʒ/" t="collocation">glass-making knowledge</T> became widespread throughout Europe and the Middle East. From the 10th century onwards, the Venetians gained a <T v="danh tiếng về kỹ năng kỹ thuật và khả năng nghệ thuật" p="/ˌrepjuˈteɪʃən fə ˈteknɪkl skɪl ənd ɑːˈtɪstɪk əˈbɪləti/" t="collocation">reputation for technical skill and artistic ability</T> in the making of glass bottles, and many of the city’s craftsmen left Italy to <T v="thành lập xưởng thủy tinh" p="/set ʌp ˈɡlɑːswɜːks/" t="collocation">set up glassworks</T> throughout Europe.

A major <T v="cột mốc trong lịch sử" p="/ˈmaɪlstəʊn ɪn ˈhɪstri/" t="collocation">milestone in the history of glass</T> occurred with the invention of <T v="thủy tinh pha lê chì" p="/led ˈkrɪstl ɡlɑːs/" t="topic-specific term">lead crystal glass</T> by the English glass manufacturer George Ravenscroft (1632 - 1683). He attempted to counter the effect of <T v="hiện tượng đục mờ" p="/ˈklaʊdɪŋ/" t="noun">clouding</T> that sometimes occurred in blown glass by introducing lead to the raw materials used in the process. The new glass he created was softer and easier to decorate, and had a higher <T v="chỉ số khúc xạ" p="/rɪˈfræktɪv ˈɪndeks/" t="topic-specific term">refractive index</T>, adding to its <T v="độ sáng và vẻ đẹp" p="/ˈbrɪliəns ənd ˈbjuːti/" t="collocation">brilliance and beauty</T>, and it proved <T v="vô giá đối với" p="/ɪnˈvæljuəbl tuː/" t="lexical pattern">invaluable to</T> the optical industry. In Britain, the <T v="ngành công nghiệp thủy tinh hiện đại" p="/ˈmɒdn ɡlɑːs ˈɪndəstri/" t="collocation">modern glass industry</T> only really started to develop after the <T v="việc bãi bỏ Đạo luật Thuế tiêu thụ" p="/rɪˈpiːl əv ði ˈeksaɪz ækt/" t="collocation">repeal of the Excise Act</T> in 1845. Owens’ invention could produce an <T v="ấn tượng" p="/ɪmˈpresɪv/" t="adjective">impressive</T> 2,500 bottles per hour. Other developments followed rapidly, but it was not until the First World War when Britain became cut off from <T v="các nhà cung cấp thiết yếu" p="/ɪˈsenʃl səˈplaɪəz/" t="collocation">essential glass suppliers</T>, that glass became part of the <T v="lĩnh vực khoa học" p="/ˌsaɪəntɪfɪk ˈsektə/" t="collocation">scientific sector</T>. Previous to this, glass had been seen as a craft rather than a <T v="ngành khoa học chính xác" p="/prɪˈsaɪs ˈsaɪəns/" t="collocation">precise science</T>.

Modern glass plants are capable of making millions of glass containers a day in many different colours, with green, brown and clear remaining the most popular. Few of us can imagine modern life without glass. It features in almost every aspect of our lives - in our homes, our cars and whenever we sit down to eat or drink. <T v="bao bì thủy tinh" p="/ɡlɑːs ˈpækɪdʒɪŋ/" t="collocation">Glass packaging</T> is used for many products, many beverages are sold in glass, as are numerous <T v="thực phẩm" p="/ˈfuːdstʌfs/" t="noun">foodstuffs</T>, as well as medicines and cosmetics.

Glass is an <T v="vật liệu lý tưởng để tái chế" p="/aɪˈdɪəl məˈtɪəriəl fə ˌriːˈsaɪklɪŋ/" t="collocation">ideal material for recycling</T>, and with growing <T v="mối quan tâm của người tiêu dùng" p="/kənˈsjuːmə kənˈsɜːn/" t="collocation">consumer concern</T> for green issues, glass bottles and jars are becoming ever more popular. <T v="tái chế thủy tinh" p="/ɡlɑːs ˌriːˈsaɪklɪŋ/" t="collocation">Glass recycling</T> is good news for the environment.
