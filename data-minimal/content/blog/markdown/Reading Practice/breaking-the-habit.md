# Annotated Passage

What is a <T v="thói quen xấu" p="/bæd ˈhæbɪt/" t="collocation">bad habit</T>? The most common <T v="định nghĩa" p="/ˌdefɪˈnɪʃən/" t="noun">definition</T> is that it is something that we do regularly, almost without thinking about it, and which has some sort of <T v="hậu quả tiêu cực" p="/ˈneɡətɪv ˈkɒnsɪkwəns/" t="collocation">negative consequence</T>. This consequence could affect those around us, or it could affect us personally. Those who deny having bad habits are probably lying. Bad habits are part of what makes us human.

Many <T v="những thói quen hình thành sớm" p="/ˈɜːli ˈhæbɪts/" t="collocation">early habits</T>, like sucking our thumb, are broken when we are very young. We are either told to stop doing it by our parents, or we consciously or subconsciously observe that others do not have the same habit, and we gradually <T v="dần từ bỏ một thói quen" p="/ɡrəʊ aʊt əv/" t="lexical pattern">grow out of it</T>. It is when we intentionally or unintentionally <T v="hình thành/thấm phải thói quen" p="/pɪk ʌp ə ˈhæbɪt/" t="lexical pattern">pick up new habits</T> in our later childhood or early adulthood that it becomes a problem. Habits that we thought we had **got rid of** can suddenly come back. During the <T v="chương trình nghiên cứu" p="/ˈstʌdi ˈprəʊɡræm/" t="collocation">study programme</T>, the researchers showed a group of volunteers several pictures, and gave them words to **associate with** them (for example, see a picture of tea, and associate it with breakfast'). They then showed the volunteers the same pictures again, and gave them new words to associate with them (see a picture of tea, and say afternoon').

A few days later, the volunteers were given a test. The researchers showed them the pictures, and told them to <T v="phản hồi bằng" p="/rɪˈspɒnd wɪð/" t="lexical pattern">respond with</T> one of the words they had been given for each one. It came as no surprise that their answers were split between the first set of words and the second. Two weeks later, they were given the same test again. This time, most of them only gave the first set of words. They appeared to have <T v="hoàn toàn quên mất" p="/kəmˈpliːtli fəˈɡɒtn/" t="collocation">completely forgotten</T> the second set.

The study confirms that the <T v="những phản ứng được học đầu tiên" p="/rɪˈspɒnsɪz wiː lɜːn fɜːst/" t="collocation">responses we learn first</T> are those that remain strongest over time. The more that response is used, the more <T v="tự động" p="/ˌɔːtəˈmætɪk/" t="adjective">automatic</T> it becomes and the harder it becomes to respond in any other way.

This is not good news for people who <T v="thói quen xấu" p="/bæd ˈhæbɪt/" t="collocation">picked up bad habits</T> early in life and now want to <T v="thay đổi hoặc từ bỏ thói quen" p="/tʃeɪndʒ ɔː breɪk ˈhæbɪts/" t="collocation">change or break them</T>.
